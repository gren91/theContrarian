The AI Delusion & Market Realities: Why CEOs Are Creating Hysteria

Contrarian Note: This analysis complements but differs from our longer-term AI infrastructure bullishness. While Tom Lee argues the AI capex boom will last decades, Steve Eisman examines near-term business deterioration and why executives are creating regulatory narratives to protect pricing power. Different time horizons, same pursuit of truth. Read both perspectives.

Steve Eisman's Sept 18 analysis cuts through the noise surrounding artificial intelligence, monetary policy, and market dynamics. His thesis: AI leaders are deliberately fostering panic about AI existentialism not to warn humanity, but to create regulatory moats that protect their pricing power during a period of slowing business growth and rising costs. This is a masterclass in market deception and misaligned incentives.

1. Long-Term Rates at 5% Are the True Market Demarcation Line

The 10-year Treasury yield has become the critical barometer for market health. Originally, Eisman thought 4.5% would break the market. He was wrong. At 5%, markets are "feeling dicey." On Wednesday, when the Fed hinted at more rate hikes, the market tanked. But on Thursday, when long-term rates fell below 5% (as investors assumed higher short-term rates would slow the economy), the market rallied back hard. This 5% level is no accident—it's where risk-free returns become so attractive that investors stop taking equity risk.

2. $500 Billion in AI Debt Is Creating a Crowding-Out Effect on Treasuries

This year alone, approximately $500 billion in AI-related long-term debt has been raised. That's enormous. This debt is competing directly with US Treasury bonds for investor capital. Some investors prefer to buy higher-yielding AI debt instead of Treasuries, creating a crowding-out effect that drives long-term Treasury yields higher. This is not just about oil prices or Fed policy—it's about capital allocation. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessant's attempt to defend rates with a $6 billion Treasury-buying program worked for exactly one day before rates marched higher again. He needs a much bigger bazooka, and even that may not be enough.

3. FICO's Monopoly in Mortgage Credit Scoring Is Breaking

Eisman has been short FICO for a while, and his thesis is playing out perfectly. FICO raised prices 1,600% over the past 5 years by leveraging its monopoly in mortgage credit scoring. The head of the FHFA (the regulator of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) has been criticizing FICO relentlessly. The pilot program using Vantage Score (the alternative to FICO) now covers ALL lenders. Year-to-date, FICO is down 43%, while Equifax and TransUnion are down 25% and 15% respectively. Vantage Score has captured 10% of new mortgage loans and this will go much higher. When the regulator agrees with the short thesis, it's usually a good sign.

4. Mortgage Insurance Companies Face Regulatory Crosshairs

Following his success with FICO, Bill Py (head of FHFA) is now going after mortgage insurers—Magic, Essent, and Radian. These companies have enjoyed decades of uninterrupted profits by charging consumers for insurance that protects the lender, not the borrower. Py is arguing these companies should be more consumer-focused and better inform borrowers when mortgage insurance is no longer necessary. This is just the beginning of a regulatory assault that will likely restructure the entire mortgage insurance industry.

5. The Clarity Act Failure Is a Major Setback for Crypto

The crypto industry invested hundreds of millions in the Clarity Act—a bill designed to create clear regulatory rules for digital assets in the US. A few weeks ago, it looked like the bill would pass, which sparked a crypto rally. But this week, senators blocked it. Democrats cited ethics concerns and a few Republicans joined the opposition. For an industry that desperately needs regulatory clarity to scale, this is a devastating blow. The crypto world is now left in regulatory limbo, with no clear path forward.

6. The AI Extinction Narrative Is a Subterfuge for Business Realities

This is Eisman's biggest and boldest claim: Dario Amodei (CEO of Anthropic) and Sam Altman (CEO of OpenAI) are deliberately spreading hysteria about AI causing human extinction not because they believe it, but because their business fundamentals are deteriorating. Here's why the "we need to slow down" narrative is pure fiction: Both companies have hundreds of billions of commitments to hyperscalers. OpenAI alone represents $300 billion of Oracle's $600 billion+ backlog. If they slow down, they can't fulfill these commitments. Anthropic is going public within months—would they tell IPO investors "growth has been great, but now we're slowing to a crawl"? The slowdown narrative contradicts their entire growth story. Elon Musk nailed it: "It's crazy 4D chess to say there's a 10% chance of annihilating humanity, but by the way, how much allocation would you like in our IPO?"

7. The Real AI Problem: Token Maxing Is Ending and Price Wars Are Coming

Business is potentially slowing because token maxing—using massive amounts of data to improve models—is hitting diminishing returns. Meanwhile, open-weight models (like Meta's LLaMA) keep taking market share. Here's the brutal truth: There are no pricing moats in this business. Today's best LLM is tomorrow's cheaper competitor. At the same time business is slowing, the cost of building data centers is rising and capital is becoming scarcer. Anthropic and OpenAI see a price war coming and need moats to defend their business. Regulation is their path to creating those moats—specifically, a regulatory framework that creates barriers to entry and fosters an AI duopoly. That's what this is really about.

8. Lying to Politicians Can Disrupt the Entire Economy

Eisman distinguishes between lying to investors (old-school Wall Street fraud) and lying to politicians (economic disruption). AI leaders' doomsday narrative has already damaged local economies. Data center regulation has become the centerpiece of many local elections. People now actually fear that the data center next door will kill them. The problem: President Trump stated this week he has no interest in regulating AI. So the entire doomsday-slowdown narrative is likely to disappear very quickly. But the damage is done. And going public while maintaining these distortions will be "very illuminating" for how the IPO process handles transparency. It's unclear how you keep a shell game going when you're required to tell the truth to investors.

The larger lesson: Markets reward truth-tellers and punish liars—eventually. Eisman's track record suggests he's worth listening to when he identifies what's "really going on" beneath the surface noise.
